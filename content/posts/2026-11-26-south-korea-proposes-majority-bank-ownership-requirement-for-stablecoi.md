# South Korea Proposes Majority Bank Ownership Requirement for Stablecoin Issuers

As of Thursday, November 26, 2026, the regulatory landscape for digital assets in South Korea is undergoing a significant transformation. Financial authorities in the country have introduced a new framework that would mandate that banks hold a stake of more than 50% in domestic stablecoin issuers. This development marks a concerted effort by regulators to institutionalize the sector and impose traditional financial stability standards on the emerging cryptocurrency payments market, according to reporting by [CryptoSlate](https://cryptoslate.com/south-korea-wants-banks-to-own-more-than-50-of-domestic-stablecoin-issuers/).

This policy shift arrives as global regulators grapple with the systemic risks posed by stablecoins—assets designed to maintain a stable value relative to a specific currency. By requiring banks, which are already subject to stringent capital requirements and oversight, to hold a majority stake in issuers, South Korean regulators are aiming to create a robust financial backstop for these digital instruments. The intention is to mitigate the liquidity or solvency risks that have historically affected independent stablecoin operators in other global jurisdictions.

## Strengthening Oversight Through Institutional Integration

Under the proposed rules, while banks are expected to maintain majority control, the framework allows for fintech firms to hold the largest individual stake in a stablecoin issuer, provided that the overarching banking ownership requirement is satisfied. This structure suggests a collaborative regulatory approach where the technological innovation of fintech companies is balanced by the financial stability and regulatory compliance of traditional banking institutions.

Furthermore, the proposals address the critical issue of what happens during a corporate failure. The draft guidelines indicate that reserve managers would be empowered to repay stablecoin holders directly if an issuer were to fail. This is a significant distinction in the regulation of stablecoins, as it directly addresses the "de-pegging" risk that often occurs when an issuer lacks sufficient, liquid reserves to meet redemption demands. By establishing a direct line of repayment, the regulators are attempting to insulate both retail and institutional users from the operational risks inherent in the underlying blockchain infrastructure.

## Contextualizing the Regulatory Strategy

For years, the cryptocurrency industry has functioned in a largely decentralized or independently regulated environment. Stablecoins serve as a "liquidity bridge" between fiat currencies and decentralized finance (DeFi) platforms, representing a critical point of integration between the two systems. If a stablecoin fails, the shockwaves are rarely contained to that specific project; they often cascade across broader digital asset markets, affecting lending protocols, exchanges, and automated market makers. By forcing domestic stablecoin projects under the umbrella of banking institutions, South Korea is effectively attempting to treat stablecoins as a quasi-fiat instrument.

Banks are inherently risk-managed; they possess the existing infrastructure to perform audits, hold segregated reserves, and communicate directly with central bank clearing systems. For the broader market, this move signifies a pivot away from the early era of digital assets toward a more supervised, bank-led ecosystem. This approach reduces the reliance on autonomous smart contracts to manage reserves and places the responsibility on human institutions that are ultimately accountable to the state. This alignment ensures that stablecoin issuance remains within the scope of existing financial safeguards.

## Implications for Future Crypto Markets

This regulatory trend may lead to a bifurcated market. On one side, there may be "compliant" domestic stablecoins that carry the oversight of the banking sector, likely favored by institutional investors and retail users looking for higher security. On the other side, offshore or non-compliant issuers might continue to operate, but they could find themselves increasingly isolated from the mainstream financial system in South Korea, potentially facing restrictions on direct "on-ramping" and "off-ramping" via local bank accounts.

This development is illustrative of the broader evolution of the digital asset industry. As projects move from niche experimental technology to integrated financial products, the degree of institutional oversight is expected to increase. Investors and market participants should monitor these developments closely, as they will likely dictate which platforms remain accessible and viable within the South Korean financial jurisdiction. As with all developments in the volatile digital asset market, it is important to note that regulatory environments are subject to change. The implementation of these proposed rules may involve further public consultation or technical adjustments.
